The Access List

Planning card

1. Situation

Thornfield merged two regional operations five years ago. Rebuilding the access roles over a weekend left two permissions sitting in the same role. Accounts Payable was four people; everybody did everything. The combination has been live ever since.

2. Process

Accounts Payable sets up vendors in the vendor master and runs payments weekly.

3. Business risk

One person could invent a company that does not exist and pay it real money. Nobody independent would ever have to look at it.

4. Control objective

Prevent. Ensure the ability to create or amend a vendor record is separated from the ability to pay vendors.

5. Control design

Preventive · automated · enforced by role design · monitored continuously by the GRC ruleset · output is the conflict analysis report.

6. Population definition

All users with production access to vendor master maintenance and/or payment run execution, at a defined point in time, across all relevant company codes.

7. Population source

GRC Access Risk Analysis at user level. Not role level.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm which ruleset was used and when it was last revalidated. Confirm no org unit or business process filter was applied. Confirm the analysis ran against production.

9. Sampling approach

Full population. This is a point-in-time conflict analysis, not a sample-based test.

10. Evidence plan

Manual journal entries are prepared by accounting staff. Entries meeting defined criteria route through workflow for independent review before posting.

3. Business risk

4. Control objective

Prevent. Entries meeting defined risk criteria are reviewed and approved by an appropriate independent person before posting.

5. Control design

Preventive · automated · routing enforced by workflow configuration. Two testable components: is the configuration correct, and did it operate correctly.

All manual journal entries posted during the period, across all company codes in scope --- including those below the routing threshold, because the threshold's correctness is what is in question.

FBL3N or the document tables. Manual entries are identified by document type, and the types used are client-specific configuration to be confirmed, not assumed.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm document types. Confirm which date field defines the period. Reconcile the count.

9. Sampling approach

Full population for the configuration test. Risk-based sample for operating effectiveness.

10. Evidence plan

Configuration: the current routing parameter, evidence of its value during the period, and its change history. The Rule That Never Fired

Planning card

1. Situation

An automated exception rule returned a clean result for six weeks because its data source had changed and it silently stopped checking.

2. Process

Automated business rules in GRC Process Control evaluate transactional data on a defined frequency and raise exceptions for control owner review.

3. Business risk

An automated monitoring control stops working without anyone noticing. Exceptions go unidentified and management relies on a monitoring process that is not operating.

4. Control objective

Ensure automated monitoring rules execute as scheduled, evaluate the intended population, and correctly identify exceptions.

5. Control design

You are testing a control that is itself a control test. The thing under examination is the monitoring mechanism, not the underlying business process.

6. Population definition

All scheduled executions of the rule in the period. Plus, for the injection test, transactions independently known to meet the condition.

7. Population source

The rule's execution history, and the underlying transactional data for the injection set.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the execution history covers the full period and the correct rule.

9. Sampling approach

Full execution history. Small targeted injection set.

10. Evidence plan

Three limbs. Configuration and change history --- what does it evaluate, when was it last changed, by whom, with what approval. Execution history --- did it run every time it should have. Injection or re-performance --- independently find a transaction meeting the condition and confirm the rule flagged it.

11. Execution steps

Display only. If you land on a screen with a button that changes something, do not press it.

Three facts. That is all this Book asks for. If you can find these three for one rule, you can test any automated monitoring rule on any platform.

Part A --- Fact one: what does it actually evaluate?

  1. Open GRC. Navigate to Rule Setup → Continuous Monitoring → Business Rules.
  2. Open one rule's definition.
  3. Read three things: the data source, the filter condition, and the deficiency criteria.
  4. Read the condition out loud.
  5. Now find the control description --- what the control matrix says this rule does.
  6. Compare them. A description is a claim. The rule definition is the fact. When they disagree, the rule wins and the description is your finding.
  7. Screenshot the rule definition.

Part B --- Fact two: when did it last run?

  1. Open the execution history or job monitor for that rule --- under Assessments → Monitoring Scheduler, or the job monitor.
  2. Set your date range.
  3. Record every execution: date, and result.
  4. Scheduled is not executed. A rule can sit scheduled and never fire --- a scheduling change, a failed job, a deactivated data source.
  5. Six weeks of "no exceptions" and six weeks of "did not run" produce the same silence. Only this screen tells them apart.
  6. Screenshot.

Part C --- Fact three: when was it last changed?

  1. Open the rule's change history.
  2. Record: when was the definition last modified, by whom, and with what approval?
  3. A rule correct all year and modified in November is two different controls. Your conclusion must cover both periods separately.
  4. Screenshot.

Part D --- The injection test

  1. This is the strongest evidence available and juniors never think of it.
  2. Independently identify a transaction that meets the rule's condition --- using SE16N or the relevant transactional report, not the rule itself.
  3. Confirm the rule flagged it.
  4. You are not testing whether the report is empty. You are testing whether it is capable of not being empty.

If there is no GRC Process Control

  1. /nSM37. Enter the job name for the equivalent monitoring job. Set the date range. Execute. That is fact two.
  2. Open the job → Step list. It shows the program it runs. That is fact one.
  3. /nSE16N on the relevant configuration table, with change documents if available. That is fact three.
  4. Say the substitution out loud in your workpaper. The tool changes at every client. The three questions do not.

What to capture

  • The rule definition, showing data source and condition
  • Execution history for the period
  • Change history, with dates and who
  • Your one-line statement of what the rule evaluates, in your own words
  • Evidence of the injection test, if you ran one

12. Evaluation

The rule's data source was changed. From that date it evaluated an empty or wrong population and returned clean. Six weeks of meaningless clean results.

A control description is a claim. The rule definition is the fact. When they disagree, the rule wins and the description is your finding.

13. Conclusion

Model

The automated monitoring control did not operate effectively from \[date\] to \[date\]. Separately: changes to monitoring rule configuration were not subject to review or approval, so the failure could recur without detection. Two findings, one root cause --- remediating only the first fixes this rule and leaves every other rule exposed.

14. Reviewer challenge

"You have told me the rule produced no exceptions. How do you know it would have produced one if there had been one?"

15. Common errors

  • Testing the output instead of the rule. An empty report and a broken rule look identical.
  • Assuming scheduled means executed.
  • Not checking when the rule was last changed. A rule correct all year and modified in November is two different controls, and the conclusion must cover both periods.
  • Never attempting an injection test. It is the strongest evidence available and juniors never think of it.

Six weeks of clean results from an automated rule. Is that good news?

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

Unknown until you establish the rule was capable of producing a dirty result. A working control that finds nothing and a broken control produce identical output. Three routes settle it: read the rule's current configuration and change history, confirm it actually executed on schedule, and independently find a transaction meeting the condition to confirm it was flagged. You are not testing whether the report is empty. You are testing whether it is capable of not being empty.

Planning card

1. Situation

A cybersecurity incident was detected and contained competently by IT Security. It then sat for twenty-three days before reaching Legal.

2. Process

Security incidents are detected, triaged and contained by IT Security. Incidents meeting defined criteria escalate to a disclosure committee, which assesses materiality and triggers reporting.

3. Business risk

A material incident is not escalated for materiality assessment in time. The company fails to make a required disclosure within the mandated period --- and the certification the CEO and CFO signed personally is false.

4. Control objective

Ensure incidents meeting defined escalation criteria reach the disclosure committee without unreasonable delay, and that materiality determinations are documented.

5. Control design

The design test is brutal and simple: do documented escalation criteria exist, and are they specific enough to be actionable by the person applying them at 3am? "Escalate significant incidents" is not a criterion. It is a hope.

6. Population definition

All security incidents logged in the period.

7. Population source

The security incident management system. Not the disclosure committee's minutes, which by definition contain only the incidents that were escalated.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Full population where volume allows. Otherwise all incidents meeting escalation criteria.

Incident records with detection, containment and escalation timestamps. Escalation criteria documentation. Disclosure committee records. For any incident meeting the criteria, evidence of the materiality determination and its date.

The Signed-Off Mitigation

Planning card

1. Situation

Calder Ridge's Accounts Payable team has carried a documented SoD conflict for two years: several users can both maintain vendor master records and execute the weekly payment run. When GRC Access Control first flagged it, a mitigating control was signed off -- the AP Manager reviews vendor master changes weekly -- and that sign-off has been renewed every quarter since, without a single exception noted.

2. Process

Accounts Payable staff set up and amend vendor records and run payments weekly; the mitigating control is a manager review meant to catch anything inappropriate before money leaves the company.

3. Business risk

If the person reviewing the mitigation isn't actually examining the changes being made, a fictitious or altered vendor could be paid and nobody independent would ever catch it -- the safety net exists on paper only.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm the mitigating control -- weekly manager review of vendor master changes -- is actually being performed against the right population, not simply re-signed each quarter.

5. Control design

Detective · manual · weekly, performed by the AP Manager · output is a signed mitigation-review log.

6. Population definition

All vendor master maintenance transactions (creates and changes) made by any user who holds both vendor-master and payment-run access, across the period under test.

7. Population source

GRC Access Risk Analysis at user level, to identify who actually holds the conflict -- cross-referenced against an independent CDHDR change-log pull. Not the mitigation owner's own summary of "changes reviewed."

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the Access Risk Analysis ruleset used matches the P2P vendor-master/payment-run risk ID, confirm the organizational-unit filter matches Accounts Payable's actual current structure rather than a stale cost-center list, and confirm the CDHDR pull covers the full review period with no date gaps.

9. Sampling approach

Full population for identifying who holds the conflict (a point-in-time analysis). For the change-log walkthrough, a judgmental selection weighted toward any change with no visible follow-up in the mitigation log.

10. Evidence plan

A dated Access Risk Analysis export naming the specific conflict-holders; the mitigation record with its last review date; an independent CDHDR export for the same period; and interview notes comparing what the reviewer says they check against what the log actually contains.

11. Execution steps

Display only. If you land on a screen with a button that changes something, do not press it.

Part A -- Confirm the conflict and pull the true population

Before you start: Confirm your system and client before touching anything -- System -> Status, screenshot once.

  1. Open the Fiori Launchpad and search the App Finder for "Access Risk Analysis" (or in NWBC: GRC -> Access Management -> Access Risk Analysis) if it isn't already pinned.
  2. Set Analysis Type = User Level. Not Role Level -- role level tells you which roles are internally conflicted, not who holds them.
  3. Use the risk field's value-help to select the P2P vendor-master/payment-run risk ID.
  4. Restrict Organizational Unit to Accounts Payable and click Search/Execute.
  5. Screenshot the selection screen before you execute -- the export will not contain it.
  6. Export the results list (Export icon, top-right of the results toolbar -> Excel). This flagged-conflict list is your test population.

What to capture: Selection-screen screenshot · Exported conflict-holder list (Excel)

Part B -- Pull the mitigation record

  1. Open the Rule Book / Mitigation Assignment app from the GRC Fiori Launchpad or NWBC.
  2. Search for the Mitigation ID tied to this risk and open the record.
  3. Capture the control description exactly as written, and the last documented review date.

What to capture: Mitigation record screenshot showing description and last review date

Part C -- Independently verify what was actually reviewed

  1. Type SE16N in the command field, press Enter, and open table CDHDR.
  2. Restrict the object class to vendor master and the date range to the claimed review period covered by the sign-off.
  3. Execute and export the change log -- this is your independent population, pulled without the mitigation owner's involvement.
  4. Compare the count and content of this export against what the mitigation log claims was reviewed.
  5. Interview the mitigation owner. Ask them to walk through exactly what they check each week -- don't lead them toward "the full change list."
  6. Write down their answer verbatim, then compare it against the independent CDHDR export.

What to capture: Independent CDHDR export · Interview notes, verbatim where possible

12. Evaluation

The conflict-holder population comprises six users in Accounts Payable. The mitigation log shows a signed weekly review for thirteen consecutive quarters with zero exceptions noted. The independent CDHDR pull for the same period shows thirty-four vendor master changes made by conflict-holders. When interviewed, the reviewer described checking "a couple of recent ones" each week -- not the full change list.

13. Conclusion

Model

The mitigating control is not operating effectively. The reviewer is not examining the actual population of vendor master changes made by conflict-holders: thirty-four changes occurred against zero documented exceptions across thirteen quarters, and the reviewer's own description of their process does not match a full-population review.

14. Reviewer challenge

"Thirteen quarters with zero exceptions is a pretty strong track record -- why isn't that good evidence the control works?"

15. Common errors

  • Accepting a consistent sign-off history as evidence of operation, without ever independently pulling the underlying population.
  • Treating "the reviewer says they check it" as sufficient, without testing what they actually check against real data.
  • Running Access Risk Analysis at role level instead of user level, which understates who actually holds the conflict.
  • Confusing "the mitigation is documented" with "the mitigation is operating."

Gate Facilitator only

The AP Manager says: "I've done this exact review every week for two years. Are you telling me my quarterly sign-off means nothing?" Do you accept that?

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

No -- and this isn't about doubting the manager's diligence, it's about testing what the review actually covers. A sign-off is an assertion; the test is whether the underlying population was actually examined. The interview and the independent change-log pull either confirm or contradict that assertion. The manager's tenure and good faith aren't the question -- the completeness of what they're checking is.

The Standing Firefighter

Planning card

1. Situation

Three Firefighter (superuser privileged access) IDs are active in Calder Ridge's production system. One was assigned eleven months ago for a specific finance-close cutover project that finished in a week. Nobody has revisited the assignment since -- there is no expiration date on file and no periodic re-justification requirement in the current process.

2. Process

Firefighter access grants temporary elevated authorization for emergency or project work, and every session is meant to be logged and independently reviewed afterward by a named controller.

3. Business risk

Someone could use standing elevated production access to make an unauthorized change to financial data or configuration, and if nobody is actually reviewing the sessions, nobody would ever look.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm every Firefighter ID's assignment, usage, and logging is being reviewed in line with policy -- not left standing open indefinitely because the original justification is long since finished.

5. Control design

Detective · periodic · performed by a named Firefighter controller · output is a reviewed session log with disposition per session.

6. Population definition

All active Firefighter ID assignments in the production system, and every session logged against each ID within the test period.

7. Population source

GRC Superuser Privilege Management (GRAC_SPM) assignment list and session log -- not a manager's recollection of who "should still have it."

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the GRAC_SPM export reflects the current production assignment list (not a stale snapshot), and confirm the SM20 pull covers exactly the same date range and same IDs as the session log, so nothing executed is missed.

9. Sampling approach

Full population of active Firefighter assignments (there are only a handful; no sampling needed). Full population of sessions within the test period for each ID in scope.

10. Evidence plan

The Firefighter ID assignment list with owners and any expiration dates; the GRAC_SPM session log for the test period; the corresponding SM20 security audit log for the same period and IDs; and confirmation of whether a compensating periodic review exists for assignments with no expiration date.

  2. Go to the ID assignment / owner view.
  3. Export the current list of Firefighter IDs with assigned owners and expiration dates (where any exist).

What to capture: GRAC_SPM assignment export

  2. Filter by the test period and by the Firefighter IDs in scope.
  3. Export the session-level log: who logged in, when, and for how long.

  3. Execute and review the transaction-level detail for each session.
  4. Compare what SM20 shows was actually executed against what, if anything, the Firefighter log's disposition/review notes say was checked.
  5. For any assignment with no expiration date, confirm whether a compensating periodic review process exists at all. If none does, document the specific assignment and how long it has stood.

One has an expiration date tied to a specific quarterly close window; the other two -- including the one assigned eleven months ago for a finished cutover project -- have no expiration date and no periodic re-justification on file. 13. Conclusion

The Firefighter control is not operating effectively for the two IDs with no expiration date. Nineteen unreviewed sessions is the same exposure regardless of who is behind the login -- the control is meant to produce evidence of oversight independent of anyone's reputation, and right now it produces none.

The Redesign That Broke Something Else

Planning card

1. Situation

Six months ago, Calder Ridge's security team redesigned a Finance role to remediate a flagged SoD conflict between GL account maintenance and journal entry posting. The redesign was approved, documented, and closed as remediated. Nobody has re-run a full risk analysis against the redesigned role since.

2. Process

IT Security modifies role authorizations through PFCG following an approved design document, then GRC Access Control is meant to confirm the specific conflict was removed.

3. Business risk

A role redesign that removes one conflict can add authorization objects that create a completely different conflict -- and if only the original conflict is retested, the new one goes live undetected.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm the redesigned role does not contain any new SoD conflict introduced by authorization objects added during the remediation -- not just that the original conflict is gone.

5. Control design

Detective · one-time per change · performed by GRC Access Control analyst · output is a post-change risk analysis report.

6. Population definition

Every authorization object added to or removed from the redesigned role between its pre-redesign and current versions.

7. Population source

PFCG role change history (the authoritative source of what actually changed), not the original redesign's approved design document, which only shows what was intended.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the PFCG change history covers the full period from immediately before the redesign to today, with no gaps from subsequent unrelated changes muddying the comparison.

9. Sampling approach

Journal entries above a defined dollar threshold require manager approval before posting; entries below it post directly.

Full population pull, followed by full review of every cluster identified through aggregation -- clustering by definition can't be sampled for, since a partial pull could miss the pattern entirely.

The Role That Outlived Its Purpose

Planning card

1. Situation

A composite role built years ago for a since-dissolved team is still assigned to a dozen people at Wrenfield. The role has accumulated authorization objects across several redesigns since the Castellane Systems merger, none of them individually risk-assessed against the role's original design intent.

2. Process

Roles are meant to be built and maintained against a defined job-function scope; over time, ad hoc additions during unrelated projects can quietly expand what a role grants well beyond that original scope.

3. Business risk

A role can drift authorization by authorization, each addition individually harmless-looking, until the accumulated total contains a SoD conflict nobody deliberately created or approved.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm the role's current authorization content still matches its original design intent, and that accumulated scope drift has not introduced an undetected conflict for anyone currently assigned it.

5. Control design

Detective · periodic · performed by GRC Access Control analyst · output is a role drift and current-state risk analysis report.

6. Population definition

Every authorization object added to the role since its original creation, and every individual currently assigned it.

7. Population source

PFCG role change history (the authoritative record of what was actually added, and when) -- not the role's current authorization list alone, which shows the end state but not how it got there or whether each addition was ever assessed.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the PFCG change history pull covers the role's full lifetime back to original creation, not just a recent window, so early-stage drift isn't missed.

9. Sampling approach

Full population -- the role's change history is a discrete, fully reviewable list, and every addition is a testable item in its own right.

10. Evidence plan

The full PFCG change history showing every object added chronologically; the role's original design/job-scope document; a current Access Risk Analysis at role level; and a user-level Access Risk Analysis for everyone currently assigned the role.

11. Execution steps

Display only. If you land on a screen with a button that changes something, do not press it.

Part A -- Run current-state risk analysis

Before you start: Confirm system/client, screenshot System -> Status once.

  1. Open Access Risk Analysis from the Fiori Launchpad (App Finder search if not pinned) or NWBC: GRC -> Access Management -> Access Risk Analysis.
  2. Set Analysis Type = Role Level, enter the role name, and click Search/Execute.
  3. Screenshot the result before moving to the change history -- you need a current-state baseline to compare against.

What to capture: Role-level Access Risk Analysis current-state result

Part B -- Pull the full change history

  1. Type PFCG, press Enter, enter the role name.
  2. Click Display (glasses icon) -- never Change.
  3. Open Change Documents / history from the role's menu.
  4. List every version's delta chronologically, back to the role's original creation.

What to capture: Full PFCG change history, chronological

Part C -- Trace drift against original scope, then retest at user level

  1. Compare the accumulated changes against the role's original job-scope or design document.
  2. Flag every authorization object added that falls outside that original scope and was never separately risk-assessed at the time.
  3. Re-run Access Risk Analysis at user level, filtered to everyone currently assigned the role, to confirm whether the drifted scope has created a live conflict for any specific person.

What to capture: Scope-drift comparison against original design document · User-level Access Risk Analysis for all current role holders

12. Evaluation

The role has accumulated eleven authorization object additions since original creation, four of which fall outside its documented original scope and were never separately risk-assessed. A user-level risk analysis surfaces one live SoD conflict for a single current holder, traceable directly to one of the four out-of-scope additions.

13. Conclusion

Model

The role's scope has drifted materially beyond its original design intent, and that drift has produced a live conflict for at least one current holder. A role that has never triggered a rule violation historically is not the same as one whose scope has stayed constant -- the conflict only became visible once the full change history was reconciled against current assignments.

14. Reviewer challenge

"This role has been in place for years without a single flagged violation until now -- why should we treat this as a systemic issue rather than one overlooked addition?"

15. Common errors

  • Relying on the current-state risk analysis alone, without pulling the change history to see how scope accumulated over time.
  • Assuming a role with no historical violations has a stable, unchanged authorization footprint.
  • Comparing accumulated changes only informally instead of against the role's actual original design/job-scope document.
  • Treating the single flagged conflict as the full scope of the issue without checking whether other out-of-scope additions carry similar risk for future assignees.

Gate Facilitator only

Role Design says: "Every one of those eleven additions was approved through a change ticket at the time -- how is this our process failing?"

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

Individual change tickets approving individual additions is exactly how scope drift happens invisibly -- each approval evaluated one addition in isolation, never the role's cumulative authorization footprint against its original design. The process gap isn't that changes were unapproved; it's that no step in the approval process asked whether the *role as a whole*, after the change, still matched its intended scope. That's a different, and more fixable, finding than blaming any single ticket.

The ID With Five Faces

Planning card

1. Situation

One login is shared by five people on the Castellane integration team, adopted as a convenience during the post-merger transition period. Every action taken under that ID is, on paper, attributed to nobody in particular.

2. Process

Individual accountability for system actions depends on each user having their own unique login; a shared ID breaks that link entirely for every action performed under it.

3. Business risk

If something goes wrong under a shared ID -- an error, an unauthorized change, a fraud -- there is no way to determine which of the five people actually did it, which defeats every downstream control that assumes actions can be traced to an individual.

4. Control objective

Detect. Determine whether activity performed under the shared ID can be reliably attributed to a specific individual, and identify precisely where that attribution breaks down.

5. Control design

Detective · one-time assessment with ongoing implication · performed by Security/Access analyst · output is an attribution-gap report per sampled action.

6. Population definition

Every sensitive transaction logged under the shared ID during the test period.

7. Population source

SM20 security audit log for the shared ID -- corroborated where possible against independent sources (password-reset records, ticketing access logs, physical access logs) since SM20 alone can only ever show which ID acted, never which person.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the SM20 pull covers the full test period and all relevant audit classes, and confirm the list of people with knowledge of the shared credentials is itself complete (not just the obvious core team).

9. Sampling approach

Judgmental sample weighted toward the most sensitive transaction types logged under the ID, since these carry the highest consequence if attribution genuinely cannot be established.

10. Evidence plan

The SM20 audit log export for the shared ID; the roster of everyone with knowledge of or access to its credentials during the period; and, for each sampled sensitive action, a documented attempt (successful or not) to independently corroborate who actually performed it.

11. Execution steps

Display only. If you land on a screen with a button that changes something, do not press it.

Part A -- Pull the shared ID's activity log

Before you start: Confirm system/client, screenshot once.

  1. Type SM20, press Enter.
  2. Enter the shared ID in the User field, set the test-period date range, select the relevant audit classes, and execute.

What to capture: SM20 export for the shared ID, full test period

Part B -- Establish who actually had access

  1. Pull password-reset records, ticketing system access logs, or team rosters covering the test period.
  2. Build the full list of individuals who had knowledge of or access to the shared ID's credentials -- not just the officially assigned five.

What to capture: Roster of everyone with credential knowledge/access during the period

Part C -- Attempt independent corroboration on a sample

  1. Select a sample of sensitive transactions from the SM20 log, weighted toward the highest-consequence types.
  2. For each, attempt to corroborate the actual individual using supplementary evidence: physical access logs, calendar records, or corroborating timestamps from an adjacent system.
  3. Document each sampled action as attributed or not attributed -- do not assume the most likely person performed it without actual corroborating evidence.

What to capture: Corroboration attempt and result for each sampled action

12. Evaluation

Of twelve sampled sensitive transactions under the shared ID, corroborating evidence independently confirms the actual individual for seven. The remaining five, including two involving master-data changes, cannot be attributed to a specific person with the available evidence -- multiple team members had both credential access and physical/system presence during the relevant windows.

13. Conclusion

Model

Individual attribution is not achievable for a material portion of sensitive activity performed under the shared ID -- five of twelve sampled actions, including two master-data changes, remain genuinely unattributed. This is an open control gap, not a documentation formality: any control downstream that assumes traceable individual accountability for these actions currently has none.

14. Reviewer challenge

"We know who's on that team and it's a small group we trust -- does it really matter that we can't pin down exactly which one of the five did each thing?"

15. Common errors

  • Treating a small, trusted team as a substitute for actual individual attribution.
  • Assuming SM20 alone can establish who performed an action, when it can only ever show which ID was used.
  • Skipping the attempt at independent corroboration and simply noting "shared ID, can't determine" without trying available supplementary evidence.
  • Letting the most plausible name in an adjacent record substitute for genuine corroboration.

Gate Facilitator only

Someone on the integration team says: "It's five people, not five hundred -- if something ever actually went wrong we could just ask around and figure out who did it."

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

"Asking around after the fact" is not a control -- it depends entirely on people remembering accurately and being honest about it, with no independent verification at all. The finding isn't a hypothetical worst case; it's that right now, for five of twelve sampled sensitive actions, there is no way to establish who performed them even with a genuine, good-faith attempt at corroboration. The fix is straightforward -- individual logins -- and the finding stands regardless of how much trust exists on the team, because the control gap is structural, not a reflection on anyone's character.

The Approver Who Never Looked

Planning card

1. Situation

Every access request at Wrenfield gets approved, and approved quickly. The approval timestamps look excellent. Nobody has checked whether the approver is actually reviewing what's being requested against the requester's role, or simply clearing the queue.

2. Process

Access requests route through a GRC approval workflow requiring a named approver to review the requested access against the requester's business justification before granting it.

3. Business risk

An approver who clears requests without genuine review provides no actual check at all -- access can be over-provisioned indefinitely with a fully documented, timestamped approval trail that looks exactly like a working control.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm that completed access request approvals reflect genuine review of the requested access against the requester's role -- not a rubber-stamp completed quickly and on schedule.

5. Control design

Detective · per-request, tested periodically · performed by GRC Access Control analyst · output is a substantive-review exception report.

6. Population definition

All completed and approved access requests within the test period, for the approver(s) in scope.

7. Population source

The Access Request app's completed-request log -- independently re-assessed against the requester's actual role and business justification, not the workflow's own "approved" status, which only confirms a step occurred.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the sample pulled covers requests of varying complexity (single-line and multi-line-item requests), since approval-time scrutiny is most informative when compared against how complex the request actually was.

9. Sampling approach

Judgmental sample weighted toward requests approved unusually quickly relative to their complexity, and toward any approver with a pattern of rapid approvals.

10. Evidence plan

Sampled Access Request records including submission and approval timestamps and stated business justification; an independent Access Risk Analysis of the access actually granted; and a comparison of approval elapsed time against request complexity.

11. Execution steps

Display only. If you land on a screen with a button that changes something, do not press it.

Part A -- Pull completed approvals

Before you start: Confirm system/client, screenshot once.

  1. Open the Access Request (or My Access Requests) app from the Fiori Launchpad, or NWBC: GRC -> Access Management -> Access Requests.
  2. Filter by status = Approved and the test-period date range.
  3. Export the resulting list including submission and approval timestamps.

What to capture: Completed Access Request export with timestamps

Part B -- Independently assess what was actually granted

  1. For each sampled request, open Access Risk Analysis at user level for the requesting individual.
  2. Filter to the access granted through the sampled request.
  3. Compare it against the stated business justification on the request record.

What to capture: Access Risk Analysis result per sampled request, compared to stated justification

Part C -- Test the approver's actual review time

  1. Calculate the elapsed time between request submission and approval for each sampled item.
  2. Flag any multi-line-item request approved in under a minute for closer scrutiny.
  3. Where granted access exceeds what the justification supports, document the specific over-provisioned access and check whether this pattern repeats across other requests approved by the same approver.

What to capture: Approval elapsed-time analysis · Documented over-provisioning instances, by approver

12. Evaluation

Of fifteen sampled requests, four multi-line-item requests were approved in under ninety seconds. In three of those four, the access ultimately granted includes at least one authorization not supported by the stated business justification. All three over-provisioned instances were approved by the same individual.

13. Conclusion

Model

The approval control is not providing genuine review for at least a subset of requests -- specifically, complex requests approved implausibly fast by one approver, three of which resulted in access exceeding what was justified. The completed workflow with a timestamp and approver name is evidence a step occurred, not evidence a substantive decision was made.

14. Reviewer challenge

"This approver processes a high volume of requests and has years of experience -- isn't it possible they can genuinely assess a request that fast?"

15. Common errors

  • Accepting a completed, timestamped approval as evidence of substantive review without independently checking what was actually granted.
  • Not comparing approval elapsed time against request complexity, which would have surfaced the pattern immediately.
  • Testing only whether an approval occurred, rather than whether the specific access granted matches the justification.
  • Treating experience or seniority as a substitute for actually verifying review quality.

Gate Facilitator only

The approver says: "I know this role and what it needs cold -- I don't need to read every line item to know if it's appropriate." Do you accept that?

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

Familiarity with a role in general is a reasonable starting point, but it doesn't explain three specific instances where the access actually granted exceeded what the stated justification supported -- that's not a matter of approval speed, it's a substantive mismatch between what was requested and what was appropriate. The test isn't questioning whether the approver is generally competent; it's pointing at three concrete, documented over-grants that a genuine review, fast or not, should have caught.

The Rollout That Rolled Back

Planning card

1. Situation

A configuration change deployed across Wrenfield's shared-services environment had to be rolled back after causing an issue for one of the entities it served. The rollback transport imported cleanly according to the technical log, and the team moved on.

2. Process

Changes in a shared-services environment affect multiple legal entities simultaneously; a rollback reverses a change but is itself a change requiring the same rigor -- approval and verification -- as the original.

3. Business risk

A rollback treated as automatically pre-approved because it reverses something already approved, and verified only by a single successful technical import, can leave some entities in the shared environment in an incorrect or inconsistent state that nobody checked individually.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm the rollback transport received independent approval with the same rigor as the original change, and that the actual post-rollback state is verified correct for every affected entity individually -- not assumed uniform.

5. Control design

Preventive (approval) and detective (verification) · per-occurrence · performed by Change Advisory reviewer · output is an entity-by-entity post-rollback state confirmation.

6. Population definition

Every entity served by the shared-services environment that was affected by the original change and its subsequent rollback.

7. Population source

The STMS transport log identifying the rollback and its linked original change -- verified against each entity's actual current configuration state independently, not inferred from the rollback transport's single successful import status.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the full list of entities served by the shared environment is used as the population, not just the entity where the original issue was reported, since a shared change affects all entities regardless of where the problem surfaced.

9. Sampling approach

Full population -- every affected entity's post-rollback state is independently checkable and the population is small enough not to require sampling.

10. Evidence plan

The STMS transport log for both the original change and the rollback; the rollback's independent approval record; and an entity-by-entity confirmation of actual current configuration state against the intended reversion target.

11. Execution steps

Display only. If you land on a screen with a button that changes something, do not press it.

Part A -- Identify the rollback and its original change

Before you start: Confirm system/client, screenshot once.

  1. Type STMS, press Enter.
  2. Open the import queue or transport log for the shared-services environment.
  3. Locate the rollback transport by its description or linked reference to the original transport.
  4. Open its object list to see exactly what the rollback touches.

What to capture: STMS transport log showing rollback and linked original transport

Part B -- Confirm independent approval

  1. Pull the rollback transport's own approval record.
  2. Confirm it received independent review and approval -- not automatic clearance because it reverses an already-approved change.

What to capture: Rollback transport's independent approval record

Part C -- Verify actual state, entity by entity

  1. List every entity served by the shared-services environment affected by the original change.
  2. For each entity, independently confirm the actual current configuration or program state -- not assume a single successful import confirms uniform reversion.
  3. Document any entity where the post-rollback state does not match the intended reversion target.
  4. For any mismatch found, trace whether it was caught by the standard change process or only surfaced here.

What to capture: Entity-by-entity post-rollback state confirmation · Note on how any mismatch was or wasn't caught previously

12. Evaluation

The rollback transport shows a single successful technical import with no separate approval record distinct from the original change's approval. Of five entities served by the shared environment, four show the intended pre-change configuration restored; one entity's configuration still reflects a partial application of the original change, undetected until this independent check.

13. Conclusion

Model

The rollback was not independently approved and was not verified at the entity level -- a single technical import success was treated as sufficient for all five entities, when in fact one entity's configuration was left in an inconsistent state that the standard process never caught.

14. Reviewer challenge

"The rollback undid exactly what the original transport did -- how could it possibly affect entities differently?"

15. Common errors

  • Treating a rollback as automatically pre-approved because it reverses an already-approved change.
  • Assuming a single successful technical import confirms a uniform result across every entity in a shared-services environment.
  • Verifying only the entity where the original issue was reported, rather than every entity the shared change actually touched.
  • Not tracing why the standard change process failed to catch the one entity left in an inconsistent state.

Gate Facilitator only

IT says: "Technically, a rollback transport just reverses object-level changes -- it should behave identically everywhere by definition."

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

That's true in principle for the transport mechanics, but it doesn't account for entity-specific configuration variations, timing differences in when each entity's instance picked up the rollback, or partial application if an entity's system state had already diverged before the rollback ran. "Should behave identically by definition" is exactly the assumption this test exists to check rather than accept -- and in this case, checking it found a real exception that the technical success status alone would never have surfaced.

The Qualified Opinion

Planning card

1. Situation

Wrenfield relies on a third-party hosting vendor with a clean SOC 2 report. Since the Castellane merger, Castellane's operations now also use the same vendor under a separate contractual arrangement negotiated before the merger. Nobody has confirmed whether Castellane's usage is covered by the same SOC report Wrenfield relies on.

2. Process

SOC reliance requires confirming the report's scope actually covers the specific service and entity relationship in question; a vendor serving multiple entities under different contracts may not provide uniform coverage across all of them.

3. Business risk

Assuming one entity's clean SOC opinion extends to a sibling entity's separate contractual relationship can mean the second entity is relying on controls that were never actually tested for its specific service tier or scope.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm that each entity within the combined organization relying on this vendor holds its own separately applicable SOC coverage, and that reliance is not assumed to transfer across separate contractual relationships.

5. Control design

Detective · annual (aligned to SOC report cycle) · performed by IT Risk / Vendor Management · output is an entity-level SOC coverage confirmation.

6. Population definition

Every entity within the combined organization with a contractual relationship with the vendor in scope.

7. Population source

The organization's vendor contract inventory, cross-referenced against each entity's actual SOC report -- not the vendor's own claim that "the SOC report covers all our clients," which is a marketing-level assertion, not a scope-verified one.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the contract inventory captures every entity's relationship with this vendor, including any relationship inherited through the merger that may not yet be in the primary contract-management system.

9. Sampling approach

Full population -- the number of entities relying on this vendor is small enough to review each one individually rather than sample.

10. Evidence plan

Each entity's contractual relationship/service tier documentation; each entity's own SOC report or explicit scope confirmation; and the user-entity controls section of each report reviewed against that entity's actual practice.

11. Execution steps

Part A -- Map entities to contractual relationships

  • Assuming shared physical infrastructure or a shared vendor name means shared SOC report scope.
  • Not building the population from the actual contract inventory, which would have surfaced Castellane's separate arrangement immediately.
  • Accepting a vendor's general assurance of coverage instead of requiring entity-specific scope confirmation in writing.
  • Testing only the entity that was already known to have coverage, rather than every entity with a contractual relationship to the vendor.

Gate Facilitator only

Vendor Management says: "We've never had an issue with this vendor across either entity -- does the contractual technicality really matter if nothing has actually gone wrong?"

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

The absence of an incident says nothing about whether the underlying controls were ever actually tested for Castellane's specific arrangement -- that's precisely what SOC reliance is meant to establish before relying on a vendor, not something inferred backward from a clean track record. The finding isn't that something has gone wrong; it's that the organization currently has no basis to know whether Castellane's service tier's controls have ever been independently assessed at all.

The Hardcoded Password

Planning card

1. Situation

Following the Castellane merger, Wrenfield inherited a large custom ABAP codebase with no prior code-review documentation. A full retroactive review of every program is not feasible in the time available, so the team proposes a risk-ranked, prioritized sweep instead.

Custom programs are meant to pass through code review and security scanning before production use; an inherited acquisition codebase may have been built under a different, unknown standard entirely.

Detect. Confirm a risk-ranked Code Inspector sweep of the inherited codebase covers financial-posting, payment-processing, and master-data programs first, and that any hardcoded credentials or bypass logic found are remediated.

Detective · one-time (post-acquisition) · performed by Basis Security team · output is a risk-ranked scan result set.

The full inventory of custom programs inherited from the Castellane acquisition, ranked by financial criticality.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the namespace/prefix search captures the full inherited custom program population, and confirm the prioritization criteria are applied consistently rather than informally.

9. Sampling approach

Documented, risk-based scoping rather than a full census -- financial-posting, payment-processing, and master-data programs are prioritized first, with the scoping decision itself explicitly documented rather than silently presented as full coverage.

10. Evidence plan

The risk-ranked custom program inventory with the prioritization criteria stated; the Code Inspector (SCI) scan results for the prioritized population; and remediation status for any hardcoded-credential or bypass-logic finding.

11. Execution steps

Part A -- Inventory the inherited codebase

The Bot With Nobody's Badge

Planning card

1. Situation

An RPA bot with its own service account, RPA_JE_POST_01, has been posting recurring journal entries at Draymoor for three years. Because it isn't a person, it has never been included in any periodic SoD review -- nobody has ever run an Access Risk Analysis against its account the way they would for a long-lived human user.

2. Process

Service accounts for automation are provisioned with whatever authorization the automation needs at build time; ongoing SoD review cycles are scoped, in practice, to human user IDs.

3. Business risk

A bot account holding a real SoD conflict is exactly as dangerous as a human holding the same conflict -- the account can create and approve, or post and reconcile, with nobody watching, and its non-human status is precisely why it has been invisible to the review process.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm whether the bot's service account holds an SoD conflict that a human account of equivalent authorization would already have been flagged for, and whether that conflict has been exercised.

Every authorization object held by the bot's service account, and its full three-year posting history.

Full population for the authorization review (one account, fully reviewable); full population for the posting-history test, since three years of a single account's output is a manageable, discrete dataset.

  • Assuming intent (a bot can't "decide" to commit fraud) is relevant to whether a control gap exists -- SoD risk is about the exposure a combination of authority creates, not the actor's motive.
  • Reviewing the bot's authorization only at build time, with no mechanism to catch scope creep or unused-but-still-held authority afterward.

The Model Reviewing the Model

Planning card

1. Situation

Draymoor uses a machine-learning model to flag anomalous vendor payments for review. The model was validated eighteen months ago against that year's transaction data and approved for production use. Its original validation report is still cited as the control's supporting evidence today.

2. Process

The model scores incoming vendor payments and flags outliers for human review; its validation report documents the accuracy and false-positive rate it achieved against the data available at validation time.

3. Business risk

A model's original validation describes how it performed once, against the data available then -- if the production data distribution has since shifted (new vendor categories, new payment patterns, business growth into new regions), the model may now be systematically missing a category of risk its original validation never had reason to test.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm the model's current performance against recent production data, and test specifically for drift between the data it was validated on and the data it now processes.

5. Control design

Detective · should be periodic, currently one-time-only since original validation · would be performed by Model Risk / GRC Process Control · output is a current-performance and drift analysis.

6. Population definition

The model's flagging decisions over the test period, and the underlying training-data profile compared against current production data.

7. Population source

The Model Monitoring app's current performance snapshot and the actual population of flagging decisions from SE16N -- not the original validation report, which reflects a point in time now eighteen months stale.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the current data pull spans a period recent enough to capture the model's present-day behavior, and confirm the training-data profile comparison covers every dimension likely to drift (vendor category, payment size, geography), not just overall volume.

9. Sampling approach

A sample of the model's recent flagging decisions, weighted toward categories that didn't exist or were underrepresented in the original training data.

10. Evidence plan

The model's current performance snapshot; the training-data-versus-production-data drift analysis; and the population of decisions tested against current standards, with any material drift documented as its own distinct finding.

  1. Independently obtain the model's original training-data profile.
  2. Compare it against the current production data distribution -- vendor categories, payment sizes, geographies.

Model

Material drift exists between the model's original training basis and its current operating environment, specifically in the fastest-growing segment of Draymoor's vendor base -- and that drift correlates with a lower flag rate exactly where reduced sensitivity would be most consequential.

  • Treating the original validation report as sufficient ongoing evidence of the model's current effectiveness.
  • Not testing for data drift specifically in the fastest-growing or newest segments of the population, where drift is most likely to concentrate.
  • Reasoning from "no reported miss" to "the model is still working," when a systematically lower flag rate in a blind spot would, by definition, produce no reported misses -- because nothing there gets flagged for anyone to notice.
  • Not establishing a periodic re-validation cadence as a standard control requirement for any production ML model.

The finding doesn't require an immediate full retrain as the only acceptable response; a documented interim compensating control (heightened manual review for that category) paired with a committed retraining timeline is a legitimate answer -- but doing nothing while citing engineering cost isn't, once the drift and its correlation with reduced sensitivity are both confirmed.

The Conflict That Wasn't Theoretical

Planning card

1. Situation

Two years ago, an SoD conflict in Draymoor's procurement function was formally risk-rated as acceptable, on the documented assumption that a compensating control -- a weekly independent review of vendor master changes -- would catch anything inappropriate. That same conflict has now been implicated in a realized fraud incident.

2. Process

SoD risk ratings that accept a conflict rather than remediate it are conditioned on a specific compensating control being genuinely operative; the rating is only as sound as that underlying assumption.

3. Business risk

When a risk rated acceptable on the assumption of a compensating control becomes a realized incident, the natural instinct is to simply re-rate the risk going forward -- but without testing whether the original compensating-control assumption ever actually held, the same failure mode remains invisible for every other risk rated acceptable on a similar assumption.

4. Control objective

Detect (retrospective). Confirm whether the compensating control assumed effective at the time of the original risk rating was actually operating during the period the incident occurred, to determine precisely which assumption failed.

5. Control design

Detective · retrospective, triggered by the incident · performed by GRC Access Control / Internal Audit · output is a documented root-cause conclusion on which assumption failed.

6. Population definition

The specific compensating control (weekly vendor master review) for the period spanning the incident.

7. Population source

SM20 security audit log, independently tested against the compensating control's documented performance -- not the control's own sign-off record, which is the artifact whose reliability is precisely in question.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the test period covers the full window the incident spans, not just the specific date the fraudulent transaction posted, since the compensating control's failure may have begun well before the incident itself.

9. Sampling approach

Full population -- this is a targeted retrospective test on one specific control over a defined incident window, not a population requiring sampling.

10. Evidence plan

The original SoD risk rating and its documented rationale; identification of the specific compensating control assumed effective; and an independent SM20-based test of whether that control was actually operating during the incident period.

11. Execution steps

Part A -- Pull the original rating and its assumption

Before you start: This test begins with documentation, establishing exactly what was assumed before testing whether it held.

  1. From the Fiori Launchpad, open Access Risk Analysis and pull the historical risk rating and rationale documentation for the risk ID now implicated in the incident.
  2. Identify the specific compensating control that was assumed effective at the time the risk was rated acceptable.

What to capture: Original SoD risk rating and rationale, with the compensating control identified

Part B -- Independently test whether the control was actually operating

  1. Type SM20, press Enter, set the date range covering the incident period.
  2. Execute and review the audit log for evidence the weekly vendor master review actually occurred and actually examined the relevant population.
  3. Test this independently of the control's own sign-off record -- the sign-off's reliability is what's in question.

12. Evaluation

The compensating review was performed and signed off every week during the incident period. The independent SM20 test, however, shows the review's actual scope covered only vendor master creates, not amendments -- and the fraudulent activity was executed entirely through an amendment to an existing vendor's banking details, a transaction type the review was never actually examining.

13. Conclusion

Model

The original risk rating's compensating-control assumption failed on a specific, identifiable point: the control was performed exactly as scheduled, but its actual scope never covered the transaction type that created the exposure. 14. Reviewer challenge

"The review was performed every single week without fail -- how is this a control failure rather than just bad luck that the fraud happened to use a transaction type outside scope?"

15. Common errors

  • Simply re-rating the risk as unacceptable going forward without testing what specifically caused the original assumption to fail.
  • Accepting the compensating control's consistent sign-off history as evidence its actual scope was adequate.
  • Conflating "the review was performed" with "the review covered what the risk rating assumed it covered."
  • Treating the incident as isolated bad luck rather than checking whether other risks accepted on a similar compensating-control assumption share the same scope gap.

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

Whether the specific attack vector was foreseeable in advance is a different question from whether the compensating control's actual scope matched what the risk rating assumed -- and that gap was discoverable at any point simply by comparing the review's documented procedure against the full population of vendor master change types. The finding isn't that fraud is always foreseeable; it's that the control's scope should have covered the full population of vendor master changes from the start, which would have caught this regardless of which specific change type the fraud eventually used.

The Clock Starts Again

Planning card

1. Situation

Draymoor experienced a security incident three months ago that was individually assessed as immaterial under Item 1.05 and not disclosed. Two more incidents of a similar nature have occurred since, each also individually assessed as immaterial. Nobody has assessed the three together.

2. Process

Item 1.05 requires disclosure of a cybersecurity incident determined to be material; the materiality assessment is normally performed per-incident as each one is identified.

3. Business risk

Item 1.05 materiality can be triggered by a pattern of related incidents collectively, even where no single incident individually crosses the threshold -- assessing only incident by incident can allow a genuinely material pattern to go undisclosed indefinitely, one immaterial-looking piece at a time.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm whether a pattern of related security incidents, assessed together rather than individually, meets the Item 1.05 materiality threshold -- independent of each incident's individual assessment.

5. Control design

Detective · triggered by any related incident, tested at each new occurrence · performed by Disclosure Controls / Legal · output is a separate aggregation-level materiality analysis.

6. Population definition

Every security incident within a defined lookback period that shares characteristics (attack vector, system affected, root cause) with the incident already assessed.

7. Population source

An independent SM20 pull covering a period wide enough to capture the full pattern of related incidents -- not the incident log limited to the one incident already individually assessed, which by construction would never surface the pattern.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the lookback period is wide enough to capture the full pattern, not artificially narrowed to a window that happens to exclude an earlier related incident.

9. Sampling approach

Full population of related incidents within the lookback period -- aggregation materiality specifically requires seeing the whole pattern, not a sample of it.

10. Evidence plan

The individual materiality assessment already performed for the specific incident; the independent SM20 log establishing the full population of related incidents; and a separate, explicit aggregation-level materiality analysis.

11. Execution steps

Display only. If you land on a screen with a button that changes something, do not press it.

Part A -- Pull the existing individual assessment

Before you start: This test starts with what's already been done, to establish the baseline before testing the aggregation question.

  1. Pull the individual materiality assessment already performed for the specific incident in question.
  2. Note the specific rationale for the "immaterial" conclusion, and what population it considered.

What to capture: Existing individual incident materiality assessment

Part B -- Establish the full population of related incidents

  1. Type SM20, press Enter.
  2. Set a wider date range covering the suspected pattern, not just the individually assessed incident.
  3. Execute and identify every incident sharing relevant characteristics with the one already assessed.

What to capture: SM20 log establishing the full population of related incidents

  1. Assess whether the pattern of related incidents, taken together, suggests a control weakness serious enough to be material on its own -- independent of any single incident's individual assessment.
  2. Document the aggregation analysis as its own explicit materiality question, separate from the individual-incident assessments.

What to capture: Documented aggregation-level materiality analysis

12. Evaluation

Three incidents in five months share the same root cause -- a misconfigured access control on a specific class of cloud storage bucket. Each was individually assessed as immaterial based on the specific data exposed in that instance. No aggregation-level assessment of the underlying, recurring control weakness itself has been performed.

13. Conclusion

Model

The individual assessments may each be defensible on their own narrow terms, but the pattern -- three incidents in five months from the same unremediated root cause -- has never been assessed as its own materiality question, and that aggregation-level assessment is what Item 1.05 requires when a pattern exists.

14. Reviewer challenge

"Each incident really was minor and involved different, non-overlapping data -- why should three genuinely separate minor events be treated as one material one?"

15. Common errors

  • Assessing materiality only per-incident, with no explicit step considering the pattern across incidents.
  • Narrowing the lookback period in a way that happens to exclude an earlier related incident from the population.
  • Treating "different data exposed each time" as evidence the incidents are unrelated, without examining whether the underlying control weakness is the same.
  • Not documenting the aggregation question at all, leaving no evidence the pattern was even considered rather than simply not disclosed.

Gate Facilitator only

General Counsel says: "If we start aggregating every minor incident, we'll end up disclosing constantly and creating unnecessary alarm over things that individually don't matter."

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

The aggregation test isn't "combine every incident regardless of relationship" -- it specifically applies when incidents share a common, unremediated root cause, which is exactly the case here. Three incidents from the same misconfigured control in five months isn't three unrelated minor events; it's one recurring control weakness that has manifested three times, and that's a materially different fact pattern than three genuinely independent incidents would be. The disclosure question follows from that distinction, not from a blanket aggregation policy.

The Dashboard Too Loud to Hear

Planning card

1. Situation

Draymoor's Continuous Controls Monitoring program generates an average of eleven hundred alerts per month across the organization. The CCM rules themselves are technically executing exactly as configured. Nobody has tested whether a genuinely high-risk alert can actually get meaningful attention inside that volume.

2. Process

CCM rules flag transactions or conditions meeting defined risk criteria; alerts are meant to be triaged by severity so the highest-risk items receive prompt, substantive review regardless of overall volume.

3. Business risk

A monitoring program generating alerts correctly at the rule-execution level can still fail operationally if the sheer volume of routine, low-risk alerts buries the genuinely high-risk ones -- the rules working is not the same as the right alerts actually getting looked at.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm whether high-risk alerts are structurally guaranteed to surface for review above routine alert volume, or whether they can be buried by sheer count -- testing the triage mechanism, not just individual rule execution.

5. Control design

Detective · continuous, with the triage mechanism itself as the object of this test · performed by GRC Process Control analyst · output is a triage-effectiveness assessment.

6. Population definition

The full alert population for the test period, with a focus on the subset flagged as high-risk/high-severity.

7. Population source

The CCM Alert Dashboard's full alert export -- specifically testing the actual review status of sampled high-risk alerts, not the dashboard's own "generated" count, which says nothing about whether anyone looked at them.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the alert population pulled includes every priority tier, not just high-risk alerts, since the volume of routine alerts is itself part of what's being tested (the burial mechanism).

9. Sampling approach

A sample of high-risk alerts specifically, tested for actual review status and timeliness -- the routine-alert volume is context, not itself a sampling population.

10. Evidence plan

The full CCM alert population with priority/severity for the period; the actual triage/review status and timing for sampled high-risk alerts; and the triage rule configuration itself, reviewed to confirm structural protection for high-risk items.

11. Execution steps

Display only. If you land on a screen with a button that changes something, do not press it.

Part A -- Pull the full alert population

Before you start: Confirm system/client, screenshot once.

  1. From the GRC Fiori Launchpad, open the CCM Alert Dashboard.
  2. Filter to the test period and export the full alert population, including assigned priority/severity for each.

What to capture: Full CCM alert population export, with priority/severity

Part B -- Test actual review status for high-risk alerts

  1. Identify a sample of high-risk alerts from the population.
  2. Independently trace whether each was actually triaged and reviewed within the expected timeframe -- not just generated.

What to capture: Actual review status and timing for each sampled high-risk alert

Part C -- Review the triage mechanism itself

  1. From the GRC Fiori Launchpad, open the Business Rules / Automated Monitoring app.
  2. Review the prioritization/triage rule configuration to confirm whether high-risk alerts are structurally guaranteed to surface above routine volume, or whether they can simply be buried by count.
  3. Document any high-risk alert generated correctly but never meaningfully reviewed due to volume, as a finding distinct from any rule that failed to generate an alert at all.

What to capture: Triage rule configuration review · Documented instances of high-risk alerts buried by volume

12. Evaluation

Of fourteen sampled high-risk alerts, five were reviewed within the expected timeframe; nine sat in the same undifferentiated queue as routine alerts for an average of eleven days before any review occurred. The triage configuration assigns a severity tag to each alert but does not route high-severity items to a separate, prioritized queue -- all alerts, regardless of severity, compete for review in arrival order.

13. Conclusion

Model

The CCM rules are executing correctly, but the triage mechanism does not structurally protect high-risk alerts from being buried by routine volume -- nine of fourteen sampled high-risk alerts sat unreviewed for an average of eleven days, which is a design gap in how alerts are routed, not a failure of any individual rule.

14. Reviewer challenge

"Every one of these alerts was eventually reviewed, and none of them turned out to represent an actual issue -- doesn't that suggest the delay didn't actually matter in practice?"

15. Common errors

  • Evaluating CCM effectiveness by confirming rules execute, without testing whether triage actually surfaces high-risk items promptly.
  • Treating eventual review, regardless of delay, as equivalent to timely review for high-risk items.
  • Judging the finding by whether this specific batch of delayed alerts happened to be true issues, rather than by whether the triage design reliably protects against delay when it does matter.
  • Not reviewing the triage rule configuration itself, which is where the actual structural gap is visible.

Gate Facilitator only

The Process Control owner says: "We review every alert eventually -- an eleven-day average delay on a handful of alerts isn't the same as alerts never being reviewed at all."

Facilitator model answer — do not show students

That's true, and it's exactly why this is a design finding about timeliness rather than a claim that high-risk alerts are never reviewed -- but for a control meant to catch high-risk conditions promptly, an eleven-day average delay is a materially different control than the one the program's design implies it provides. The fix is straightforward -- a genuinely separate, prioritized queue for high-severity alerts -- and the finding matters precisely because the next high-risk alert might not be one where an eleven-day delay turns out to be harmless.

The Restatement

Planning card

1. Situation

A material error in Draymoor's revenue recognition was discovered eight months after the period it affected, triggering a restatement. The immediate question from the Audit Committee is whether the control that should have caught it simply wasn't performed, or whether the control was never actually capable of catching this type of error at all.

2. Process

The Pipeline That Deployed Itself

Planning card

1. Situation

Draymoor's engineering team adopted a CI/CD pipeline that can deploy directly to the SAP production environment, bypassing the traditional STMS transport workflow entirely for certain change types. The pipeline was adopted for deployment speed; nobody has confirmed whether it has its own equivalent approval gate.

2. Process

STMS enforces a developer/approver separation by design -- a change must be transported and approved before reaching production. A CI/CD pipeline is a different mechanism entirely and does not automatically inherit that separation unless it is deliberately built in.

3. Business risk

A pipeline that can deploy directly to production collapses the developer/approver separation STMS was built to enforce, unless an equivalent gate is deliberately constructed inside the pipeline itself -- assuming the new process is just as safe because the old process used to be is exactly how this gap goes unnoticed.

4. Control objective

Detect. Confirm whether pipeline-originated production deployments have an equivalent approval gate to STMS's transport workflow, or whether they bypass developer/approver separation entirely.

5. Control design

Preventive (approval gate, if it exists) and detective (reconciliation against STMS) · per deployment · performed by Change Advisory / DevOps Security · output is a gate-existence confirmation and bypass reconciliation.

6. Population definition

Every production deployment originating from the CI/CD pipeline during the test period.

7. Population source

The CI/CD platform's own deployment log, reconciled against the STMS transport log for the same period -- specifically to identify deployments that reached production without flowing through either workflow's approval step.

8. Completeness and accuracy

Confirm the CI/CD deployment log captures every production deployment, not just those the pipeline itself categorizes as significant, since a bypass is most likely to hide in a deployment the pipeline treats as routine.

9. Sampling approach

Full population reconciliation between the two logs, since a bypass is a binary presence/absence question best answered by complete reconciliation rather than sampling.

10. Evidence plan

The CI/CD pipeline's full deployment log; the STMS transport log for the same period; confirmation of whether an equivalent approval gate exists within the pipeline itself; and documentation of any deployment bypassing both.
